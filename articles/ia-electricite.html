AI isn't short on chips. It's short on plugs.

@ghostfellah·Draft, October 21, 2026·X Article

Microsoft just walked away from a site built for 23,040 Nvidia GB300s.

Not because of chips. Not because of demand.

Because the local grid can't power it until the 2030s.

That's the real AI bottleneck. Here it is, in plain words.

1. Microsoft just left 23,040 GPUs on the table

Loughton, Essex. In September 2025, Nscale and Microsoft announced the UK's flagship AI site: 23,040 Nvidia GB300 GPUs, 50 MW of AI compute, opening in 2027. Those are the companies' own numbers. The government called it the "largest UK sovereign AI datacentre."

Then reality showed up.

In March 2026, The Guardian found the site was still a working scaffolding yard.

In September, UK Power Networks reportedly told Nscale the grid couldn't supply enough power until the early-to-mid 2030s.

On October 18, the news broke: Microsoft, the anchor tenant, had terminated its agreement. "This decision relates to a single site," a Microsoft spokesperson told Reuters. Nscale says it's still committed and is looking at alternative power sources.

The chips exist. The customer existed. The electrons don't.

Microsoft has said this out loud before. Satya Nadella, November 2025: "You may actually have a bunch of chips sitting in inventory that I can't plug in… It's not a supply issue of chips; it's actually the fact that I don't have warm shells to plug into."

2. The demand side: a Japan-sized load by 2030

Is the demand real? Yes.

The IEA says data centres used 485 TWh of electricity in 2025. Its base case for 2030: about 950 TWh. Roughly double in five years.

For scale: in 2025 the IEA said its 2030 projection was slightly more than Japan's entire electricity consumption today. A whole G7 economy's worth of power, for servers.

AI-focused data centres are the fast lane. Their consumption jumped 50% in 2025 alone, and the IEA sees it tripling by 2030.

Globally, that's only about 3% of electricity. The problem is where it lands.

In the US, data centres used 4.4% of all electricity in 2023 (Lawrence Berkeley National Lab). LBNL's latest update sees 9.5% to 15.3% by 2030. EPRI says 9% to 17%.

Then there's Virginia. Data centres already use more than 25% of the state's power. EPRI's scenarios put it at 39% to 57% by 2030.

One state. Potentially more than half its electricity going to servers.

3. The real bottleneck: the queue

A GPU ships in months. A grid connection runs on a different clock.

Berkeley Lab's Queued Up 2026: about 2,061 GW of generation and storage were waiting to connect to the US grid at the end of 2025.

The median project built in 2025 took 61 months from its interconnection request to switching on. In 2015 it took 36 months. In 2008, 22.

Most never make it. Only 13% of the capacity that asked to connect between 2000 and 2020 was running by the end of 2025.

The UK is worse in its own way. Ofgem says about 315 data centre projects are queuing to connect, for 73 GW of demand. Peak demand for the entire country is 45 GW.

The queue is bigger than the country.

My favourite example is Three Mile Island. Constellation is restarting Unit 1, now called Crane: 835 MW, with a 20-year deal to sell all of its output to Microsoft. In March 2026, Constellation said PJM's first answer was that the plant couldn't connect until 2031. It needed 765-kV and 500-kV transmission upgrades that weren't due until December 2030. A FERC waiver on June 1 put a 2027 restart back in play.

Read that again. A nuclear plant, for Microsoft, still had to wait in line.

4. Someone pays: the PJM bill

PJM runs the largest US grid: 13 states plus DC. It auctions "capacity": paying power plants to be there years ahead.

Here is the clearing price, in $ per MW-day:

  • 2024/25: $28.92
  • 2025/26: $269.92
  • 2026/27: $329.17 (cap)
  • 2027/28: $333.44 (cap)
  • 2028/29: $325.00 (cap)

Without the cap, PJM says the July 2026 auction would have cleared near $555. Cost: $16.4B, versus $29.7B uncapped.

And even at the cap, PJM still came up about 6.8 GW short of its reliability target.

Why? PJM estimates that roughly 30 GW of the 32 GW of extra peak demand it expects from 2024 to 2030 comes from data centres.

Households feel it too. Virginia's regulator approved a Dominion increase of $11.24 a month on a typical residential bill in 2026, plus $2.36 in 2027. It also created a new rate class for customers of 25 MW and up, data centres included, starting in 2027, so they carry more of the grid buildout.

The rulebook is still being written. On October 9, FERC rejected most of PJM's large-load plan, including mandatory curtailment and "bring your own capacity."

To be fair, the IEA says data centres don't automatically raise prices. On a grid with spare capacity, they can even lower them. PJM isn't that grid.

If you watch the Fed, watch this line too.

5. Big Tech's workaround: bring your own power plant (or go to orbit)

Can't get the grid? Build your own power.

Gas. GE Vernova says its gas turbine backlog and slot reservations reached 116 GW at the end of Q2 2026. It's already taking reservations for 2031 deliveries, and its CEO says about 20% of customers are data centres. Want a turbine? Get in line. Again.

The IEA thinks 15 to 27 GW of onsite gas could power data centres by 2030. But reliable onsite power needs 30% to 70% more generation than the load. And with turbines sold out, the IEA says onsite isn't necessarily faster.

Nuclear. Crane/TMI is aiming for 2027. On October 19, Amazon, X-energy and AEP announced a proposed 960 MW plant made of 12 Xe-100 small modular reactors, on a former coal site in Indiana. It's a development agreement. No final investment decision, no license, no operating date. X-energy hasn't broken ground on its first plant yet.

Orbit. Yes, really. SpaceX says each of its Starmind "AI1" satellites carries up to 250 kW peak and 175 kW average of compute, and that its factory will start producing them as soon as late 2027. Musk has said the first launch is targeted for late 2027 (Bloomberg). Google's Project Suncatcher already has a prototype TPU satellite in orbit, built with Planet, with two more planned for 2027.

Napkin math on SpaceX's own numbers: 1 GW ÷ 175 kW ≈ 5,700 satellites. Loughton's 50 MW ≈ 286 of them.

Every workaround comes with its own queue.

6. Bitcoin miners saw it first

$BTC miners spent a decade getting good at one boring thing: finding cheap power and getting it connected.

Turns out that's the scarcest asset in AI.

  • IREN announced a $9.7B, 5-year contract with Microsoft in November 2025: 200 MW of IT load in Childress, Texas, running GB300s.
  • Hut 8 announced a $7B, 15-year lease with Fluidstack for 245 MW at River Bend, to support Anthropic.
  • Core Scientific says in its filings that about 590 MW of IT capacity across five sites is contracted to CoreWeave, after its own shareholders voted down CoreWeave's takeover. In July 2026 it announced an AMD deal: more than 500 MW from 2027, up to 2.5 GW.

Those are company announcements. And a contract is not revenue.

October 14 made that point. A hot US inflation print, plus a Gartner forecast that more than 80% of neoclouds won't stay independent by 2030. Gartner also warned that over $3.5T of AI infrastructure spending could be exposed to impairment by the end of 2030.

In one day: the WGMI miners ETF fell 10.7%. IREN -12.2%. Cipher -12.3%. Hut 8 -10.5%. CoreWeave -15.5%.

$BTC itself? About -2%.

Energized megawatts are the moat. Debt is the risk. And rates decide which one wins.

7. Musk is half right

On October 20, Elon Musk:

"Lack of electricity" is an oversimplification. Everything is hard about bringing massive compute online at extremely high speed. If there was twice as much time, this would be relatively easy, but there is not.

He's right. On September 1 he listed what it takes: "massive power plants, transformers, liquid cooling loops & chillers, as well as incredibly complex networking."

The IEA backs him up. AI server power density rose 11x between 2020 and 2025, with another 4x expected by 2027. By then, a single rack the size of a fridge could have the peak power demand of 65 households. A shortage of high-bandwidth memory is expected to last at least through the end of 2027. Gas turbine orders jumped 70% in 2025.

But read his last sentence again: "If there was twice as much time, this would be relatively easy."

That's the whole thesis. Everything on the list is a time problem. And the slowest item isn't the chip. It's the wire: a 61-month median.

Follow the megawatts, not the GPUs.

In plain words: chips are a supply-chain problem. Power is a time problem.

You can't 3D-print a substation.

The next AI winners won't just have the most GPUs. They'll have megawatts already plugged in.

What I'm watching:

  • PJM and FERC rewriting the large-load rules
  • Ofgem's UK queue reform
  • GE Vernova's next turbine backlog
  • Crane/TMI, aimed at 2027
  • First Starmind launches, if SpaceX hits late 2027

Which bottleneck breaks first: the grid, the turbines, or the debt?
